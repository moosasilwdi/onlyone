Notes · how it works


      

      
      
      

        
What this is

        
        
In some diseases the immune system turns on the body itself. The attack starts
        small: one short piece of a protein gets picked up and held out to the immune
        system, like a wanted poster.

        
ONLY ONE is a free app that hunts for that piece. Give it any protein. It
        cuts it into every overlapping piece and tests every one against 117 different
        people's immune systems, looking for the rare piece that one person's body reacts
        to and almost nobody else's.

        
Jo-1 is the worked example, and it is the one loaded when the app opens.
        It is the protein attacked in one of these diseases, where the lungs and muscles pay
        the price. Cut into fifteens it makes 495 slices, so one press is about
        58,000 checks — and 61 of those 495 turn out to belong to a single
        holder. Everything on these pages is worked out from Jo-1 because it has to be
        worked out from something. Paste a protein of your own and the app does the same
        for it.

        
That difference is the whole point: a piece that everyone reacts to cannot be
        blocked without weakening normal defence against infection, but a piece belonging to
        one person can.

        
        
        
The app runs on an ordinary phone, in a browser, free. Nothing you paste ever
        leaves your device. Every scan is kept on the phone that ran it, there is no
        account and no sign-in, and no protein, name or result is sent anywhere. If you
        want to pass a run to somebody, Export writes a file and you send it yourself.

        
It is a search tool, not a diagnosis — it hands you a shortlist for a
        laboratory to test, not an answer.

        
The mission is simple: take a search that today needs a
        university, and put it in the hands of anyone willing to look.

      


      

        
The story

        
Jo-1 is a protein every one of your cells needs. In some people the
        immune system turns on it, and the muscles and the lungs pay for it.

        
The whole thing starts in a groove. One HLA molecule picks up a short
        piece of Jo-1, fifteen letters long, and holds it up for a T cell to read. Which piece gets
        picked decides everything that follows.

        
So that is the question this app was built around — and it was
        built by a designer, on a phone, because it turns out a phone can answer it.

      

      

        
Why this method

        
ONLY ONE was built by Moosa Silwadi, an architect and designer,
        after his own diagnosis of anti-Jo-1 antisynthetase syndrome in 2026. Not a laboratory
        project — one person reading, asking, and checking.

        
The reading kept arriving at the same fork. One road quiets the whole
        immune system: it works, and it costs the ordinary defences against germs. The other road
        goes after one thing only — the single groove where this disease is ordered.
        A key for one lock, instead of cutting the power to the whole building.

        
That is the method ONLY ONE was built for. It does not treat anything
        and it does not design a medicine. It narrows: hundreds of possible slices down to the few
        worth a laboratory's time, and it refuses the ones that would grip everybody else's holder too.

        
Two attempts at this idea reached trial years ago and both were stopped,
        because a peptide that sits in the groove can wake the cells it was meant to quiet. That is in
        the Warning panel below, and it is the reason the app narrows carefully instead of promising
        anything.

      

      

        
What makes it useful

        
The established tools score a piece against one HLA type. That
        is the right first question, and they answer it well.

        
This app asks the second one: does the same piece also grip
        everybody else's holder? A piece that grips many types would blunt normal defence against
        germs — so a tight score on its own is not good news. It is a warning sign waiting to be
        checked.

        
117 holders, every one of them, in about two seconds, in your own browser.
        That check is the app.

      

      

        
What is new here

        

          
The 117 check is the main move

          
Not a feature buried in a menu. It is the button under the map, and the whole app is
          arranged around it.

          
Narrowing turns the list upside down

          
Rank every slice by how few other people it grips and the order reverses: the tightest
          piece is often the worst candidate. That reversal is the finding, and it is the thing you
          cannot see by scoring one holder at a time.

          
It runs on your phone, not on a server

          
The trained networks are inside the page. No upload, no account, no waiting. Your
          sequence never leaves your phone.

          
One engine, two languages

          
Science gives you the nanomolar numbers. Abstract gives you the same answer in plain
          words. One switch, same engine underneath.

          
The groove is a real picture

          
Built on measured coordinates from PDB 1A6A, with the chosen letters laid along the
          measured path — turn it, slide it, open it full screen. Not a cartoon drawn to look
          convincing.

    3.

    4.

    5.

    6.

            feeds more attack. A circle that does not stop on its own.
    7. 
        


        
The hole in itunsolved

        
Slide a 15-bead window along 509 beads and you get 495 possible
        pieces. One of them started this. No laboratory has ever tested all 495 —
        and without knowing which piece, nothing can be aimed at anything.

        
        
The pieces overlap. The window slides one bead at a time, so
        piece 1 is beads 1–15, piece 2 is beads 2–16, piece 3 is beads 3–17,
        and the last one is beads 495–509. It is not 509 shared out into chunks of
        fifteen — it is every place the window can stand.

        
Count those places and you have the number: the window can start at
        bead 1 and no later than bead 495, so 509 − 15 + 1 = 495. The + 1
        is the first piece. A chain of five beads with pieces of three gives three pieces,
        not two: ABC, BCD, CDE.

          and they are the common, well-studied ones. If somebody’s holder is not
          among them, this app cannot answer for them.

        


        
        
Who is doing thisfour cells, and a hand

        

          
The scavengerA white blood cell with two jobs: it eats
            loose debris, then it SHOWS what it ate. It loads a piece into a groove and pushes
            that groove up onto its outer skin, so the piece sits on the outside, held open and
            facing out, to be read by touch. Its real names are macrophage and dendritic cell.
            It eats, carries one sample to a lymph node, shows it, and dies. One trip
            only.

          
          
          
The holder is its handThe groove is not another cell
            and not a copy of one — it is a part ON the scavenger, the way a hand is
            part of a person. The cell eats the protein, puts one piece in its hand, and
            holds the hand up for the judge to read. The 117 are 117 shapes of that hand:
            the same job in everybody, built to a different pattern in each person. So what
            this app rehearses is the hand, not the cell.

          
The judgeA T cell. It reads what the scavenger is
            holding up and decides. Each one recognises exactly one shape, out of millions
            patrolling. In this disease a single judge gives the wrong verdict.

          
          
The factoryA B cell. It cannot start without the judge's
            permission. Given it, it pours out antibodies for months — the anti-Jo-1 a blood
            test reads. It carries the same hand the scavenger does and shows slices too, so
            three kinds of cell hold pieces up: macrophage, dendritic cell and B cell.

          
The killerAnother white blood cell, waiting for orders.
            It destroys the cells it is pointed at. In this disease it goes into muscle and lung
            tissue, and it is the one that does most of the actual damage.

        

        
All four are white blood cells. Same family, four jobs. The whole
        decision happens between them, in a lymph node — never in the lung. The lung is only
        where the damage lands.


        
The order, and the antibodieswhat is not a cell

        
An antibody is not a cell. It is a tiny molecule, millions of them
        floating free in the blood. The B cell is the factory; the antibody is what comes off the
        line. Easy to mix up, and worth getting straight.


        The order is not an antibody either. It is a chemical signal the judge releases
        once it has read the groove, and it goes two ways: one tells factories to start making
        antibodies, the other tells killers to go to work.


        And an antibody does not destroy anything by itself. It sticks to its target and marks
        it — which tags it for scavengers to eat, blocks it from doing its job, and calls
        in other attack systems. Labelling is what brings the destruction.


        
How the killers find ita second groove

        
There is a second groove, and it is on every cell in the body —
        not only on scavengers. Each cell holds up small samples of whatever is inside it, all day,
        as a matter of routine. A lung or muscle cell therefore holds up its own Jo-1 pieces.


        That is how a killer finds it. It does not hunt Jo-1 loose in the tissue; it walks past,
        reads each cell's own window, and destroys the ones showing the piece it was ordered to
        attack.


        Two things worth being plain about. That second groove is a different shape from the
        one screened here, so the 495 and the 117 do not describe it. And how much of the damage is
        done by killers and how much by antibodies is still argued — muscle biopsies
        show killers pushed into the fibres, while antibody levels rise and fall roughly with how
        ill people are. Nobody has settled it. It matters less than it sounds for a decoy, because
        the judge sits above both: no orders, no killers sent and no factories licensed.


        
Why 15, and why 4the shape

        
The groove grips nine. It is open at both ends, so the piece runs
        through it like a belt through a buckle and about three hang out each side — nine
        plus three plus three is the fifteen.


        Of the nine, only four point down into the groove's floor. Those four are the grip.
        The others point up, and those are what the judge reads. That is why a decoy can exist at
        all: keep the four pointing down and it still fits; change one pointing up and the judge
        no longer knows it.


        
Why a changed bead can calm itthe whole idea

        
The judge does not attack Jo-1. It recognises one exact shape, and
        nothing else. Of Jo-1's 495 pieces it ignores 494 — the same protein, in front
        of it, every day, and it does nothing. That is how fussy it is.


        So change one bead that points up and the shape is one it has never met. The four pointing
        down are untouched, so it still drops into the groove. Jo-1, and not Jo-1.


        Leaving it unchanged is not an option. An unchanged piece is simply the trigger
        itself, and injecting that pours petrol on the fire.

        
Now the part that makes the whole idea possible. A judge does not fire on
        a shape alone. It fires on a shape arriving with an alarm beside it — the
        signals of infection or injury. A decoy arrives in a quiet lymph node with no alarm at all,
        again and again. A judge that keeps meeting its shape with nothing wrong concludes it is
        not a threat and shuts itself down; some flip into calming cells and tell other judges to
        stand down too.


        The aim is not to give the judge a new memory. It is to talk the old one out of its
        job — and because the decoy is so close to the real shape, that same judge then
        stays quiet for the real piece as well.

        
And here is where it bites back. The injection itself can raise an
        alarm — a sore arm, an irritated patch of skin. Then the judge sees its shape WITH an
        alarm, and fires harder than before. That is what happened in 2000. The bait worked; the
        needle shouted.


        
The road outnot done by anyone

        
    
          
  1. Test the five. Put each one in front of real immune cells from real
            patients and see which one they react to.
    2. 
          
  2. Build the decoy. Take the guilty piece and change one or two beads —
            enough that the T cell no longer reads it as the enemy, not so much that it stops
            fitting the window.
    3. 
          
  3. Fill the window. Injected under the skin. Scavengers eat it and load it, and
            the T cell leans in, reads a near-miss, and stalls instead of firing.
    4. 
          
  4. The order stops being given. No permission, no new antibody factories. The
            antibodies already made fade over months. The circle is broken at the one point where
            a decision gets made.
    5. 
        


        
It has been tried beforenever worked yet

        
Five trials have reached patients. Not one of them used a piece anybody
        had identified first.

        
    
          
  • Multiple sclerosis, 2000. Two trials of altered pieces. One made patients
            worse; the other caused allergic reactions. Both stopped.
    • 
          
  • Coeliac disease, Nexvax2. Three guessed gluten pieces. Phase 2 terminated at
            interim analysis — it did not protect anyone.
    • 
          
  • Multiple sclerosis, ATX-MS-1467. Phase 2. Lesions improved, patients did not.
            Dropped.
    • 
          
  • Type 1 diabetes, Diamyd. The furthest any of this ever got: Phase 3, with
            patients chosen by HLA type — the same family as DRB1*03:01. Interim results came
            back negative in March 2026 and the trial was stopped for futility in April
            2026.
    • 
        

        
Every one of them injected either a whole protein or a guessed piece.
        Nobody has ever run this knowing which fifteen to use. That is the one thing that
        has not been tried, and it is the only thing this app does.


        
        
What is not true yet, said plainly. The prediction engine here
        scores 0.747, and 0.5 is a coin toss. On the independent benchmark everyone is measured
        against, the best available tool scores about 0.822 — so this engine is a little
        behind the field, not far behind it. Either way, the five it names may be the wrong
        five. The body does not cut on a neat fifteen-letter
        grid either; real pieces run ragged from thirteen to twenty-five. A decoy competes for the
        groove with everything else that fits it, including pieces from real germs. Some antibody
        factories settle in bone marrow and keep producing for years without permission, and no
        decoy reaches those. Nothing here has been tested on a patient.

        
So this is not a cure and not a treatment. It is the shortlist that
        has to exist before either one can — and today it is the only one there is.

Why not just use all 495?

        
Because that is, near enough, the whole protein — and that is
        what has already been tried.

        
Cut Jo-1 into 495 overlapping slices and put them all back together and you have
        Jo-1 again. Every failed trial in the record below injected either a whole protein or
        a guessed piece of one.

        

          
It feeds the fire— one of those 495 is the
            piece already driving the attack. More of it is more of the thing you are trying
            to stop. CGP77116 was halted after 8 patients: 3 had flares caused by the
            treatment itself.

          
It quiets what you need— most of the 495 are
            pieces every holder grips. Teach the body to ignore those and you have weakened
            normal defence against infection along with the disease.

          
One slice, one holder— a piece that only
            a single holder grips reaches that holder's disease and almost nothing else.
            That is the whole bet, and it is the one thing nobody has tried.

        

      


      

      

      

        
Questions

        

          
Is this medical advice?

          
No. It is a research filter. It cannot diagnose anything and it cannot tell you anything
          about your own body.

          
          
Do I need to know my HLA type?

          
You have to choose one, because an answer is only an answer for a particular
          holder. You do not need it to be yours: pick any of the 117 and the app
          works. DRB1*03:01 is the one linked to anti-Jo-1. Changing the protein
          clears the choice, because a different protein is a different question.

          
          
Do I have all 117?

          
No. You carry a handful — half inherited from your mother, half from your
          father, and the same handful for life. The 117 here are the types other people
          carry, so a slice can be tried against a population instead of against one person.

          
Are the holders different in different cells?

          
No. Every cell in your body that shows slices — macrophage, dendritic cell,
          B cell — holds up the same set, because they all read your own DNA. The
          holder does not change from cell to cell. Only the slice in it does.

          
How would I find out which holders I have?

          
A test called HLA typing, from a cheek swab or a blood sample. A laboratory does
          it, and it is routine — it is the same test transplant matching uses.

          
Does each disease have its own holder?

          
No. What matters is a pair: a holder and a slice. One holder can be linked
          to several diseases, and one disease to several holders — coeliac runs with two,
          DQ2 and DQ8. And a holder is a risk, not a cause: most people who carry a risk type
          never get anything. The holder only decides which slices can be held up at all.

          
Where do the numbers come from?

          
The MHCnuggets class II networks, trained on measured binding data and published with
          their paper. They are predictions, not measurements.

          
Is anything I paste sent anywhere?

          
No. Everything runs inside the page on your device.

          
Why 117 and not more?

          
117 is how many class II holders have a trained network in that release. More types
          exist; they have no model yet.

          
Can I put in any protein?

          
Yes. Paste a sequence or drop a FASTA file and the whole thing is cut into fifteen-letter
          pieces and scored.

          
What counts as a good result?

          
Tight on this holder and loose on the other 116. Tight on everything is the result
          that should stop you.

          
          
“Only one holder” — one holder, or one person?

          
One holder. When the app says a slice is gripped by one holder alone, it
          means none of the other 116 grips it — not that nobody else in the world holds
          it. Anyone carrying that same type has the same hand and would grip it the same way.
          And “the other 116” is not every holder there is: about 11,910 class II
          types are named in IPD-IMGT/HLA, and only these 117 have a trained network. They do
          contain, exactly, all 27 molecules of the reference panel published for the widest
          population coverage — put at over 98% of people in every major population
          — so the 117 reach at least that far. That percentage is the panel's own,
          worked out from allele frequency data; it is a floor, not a count made here.

          
Does this design a medicine?

          
No. It narrows. It takes hundreds of possibilities down to a handful worth a laboratory's
          time — and the Warning panel below says what happened the last two times this idea
          went to trial.

Reading the answer

        
Every piece gets one of four words for how hard the
        holder grips it: very tight, tight, moderate, loose.

        
After the 117 check it also says how many other people's
        holders grip the same piece. None is the good answer. Three or more is the
        answer that should stop you.

      

      

        
Reading the numbers

        
Strength is in nanomolar (nM). Lower is tighter.
        Under 50 is very tight, under 500 tight, under 5 000 moderate, above that loose.
        Those words describe the number. Whether a holder grips a slice is judged
        differently — see below.

        
Each holder is judged on its own scale.
        The 117 holders are not comparable: across these same 495 Jo-1 slices the softest
        holder's middle value is 162 nM and the strictest is 21 329 nM — 132 times
        apart. One holder put 361 of the 495 slices under 500 nM; another put 11. So a single
        500 nM line would mostly have measured which holders are soft, not whether a slice is
        special. Each holder therefore carries its own line: a slice grips it when the slice
        falls in that holder's own tightest 5 per cent. Those lines run from 4 nM to
        2 937 nM. Jo-1's lines come from its own 495 slices and ship with the app.
        A protein you paste in now measures its OWN: about 120 of its slices, spread
        evenly, scored against all 117 holders, with each holder's two lines taken as
        percentiles of that. It happens once per protein, takes a few seconds, and the
        line under every result says which set was used. Before V9800 a pasted protein
        borrowed Jo-1's lines, which was a stand-in rather than a measurement.

        
The 117-type check returns one of three verdicts.
        Selective — it grips this holder and nothing else tightly.
        Mostly selective — one or two others.
        Not selective — three or more, which is the result that should stop you.

      

      

        
What the number is

        
Tightness is only half the question. A peptide that grips many HLA types would also blunt normal defence against germs. The button under a selected slice scores it against all 117 networks. Across the 61 Jo-1 decoys this reorders the list: the tightest are often the least selective.

        
A predicted binding strength in nanomolar from the MHCnuggets network for that HLA type, trained on measured binding data. It is a prediction, not a measurement. A lab would also run NetMHCIIpan and then test in cells. Treat a result here as a shortlist.

      

      

      

      

        
Warning — this strategy has harmed patients

        
Peptides designed to occupy the same groove have gone to trial in multiple sclerosis, and both were stopped:

        
    
          
  • CGP77116 — halted after 8 patients; 3 had disease flares caused by the treatment, with a rise in reactive T cells in blood and spinal fluid.
    • 
          
  • NBI-5788 — suspended after hypersensitivity reactions in 9% of patients.
    • 
        

        
A peptide that binds the groove can wake the very cells it was meant to quiet.

Pockets of the selected holder

        

        
Read from the IPD-IMGT/HLA protein alignments, release 3.66.0. These are the polymorphic β-chain positions that line each pocket: P1 = β85, β86; P4 = β13, β70, β71, β74; P6 = β11, β30; P9 = β9, β57. For DQ and DP only the β chain is shown; the α chain also contributes.

      

      

        
What the DR3 pockets prefer

        

          
P1
L I V M > F Y. Val85 + Val86 make it a smaller pocket than other DR types. Charged, G, P excluded.

          
P4
D strongly, then E. Lys71 + Arg74 make it electropositive, so K and R are shut out.

          
P6
K R H. Electronegative. Small polar residues also sit here and leave P9 open.

          
P9
No published residue list for DR3. Electronegative (Glu9, Asp57), so acidic disfavoured.

        

        
Geluk 1994 gives two alternative submotifs. This rule-based reading is a cross-check on the network; across 285 Jo-1 variants the two agree only weakly (Spearman 0.17), so the network is what the ranking uses.
